# Fundamental Analysis – Key Concepts, Formulas, and Usage Guides

## 1. Profitability Metrics

### 1.1 Net Profit Margin

**Meaning**  
Net profit margin shows how much profit a company makes for each unit of revenue after all expenses and taxes are paid.

**Formulas**

- Net Profit Margin (fraction):  
  Net Profit Margin = Net Income / Revenue

- Net Profit Margin (percentage):  
  Net Profit Margin (%) = (Net Income / Revenue) * 100

**Example**

- Net Income = 10,000,000  
- Revenue   = 100,000,000  

Net Profit Margin = 10,000,000 / 100,000,000 = 0.10 = 10%

**How to Use (Investing vs Trading)**

- Investing: Prefer stable or rising margins, ideally above the industry average. Sustained margin expansion is a strong quality signal.  
- Trading: Watch for margin expansion or compression around earnings; big surprises can drive strong post‑earnings moves.


### 1.2 Return on Equity (ROE)

**Meaning**  
Return on equity measures how much profit a company generates from the shareholders’ equity (the shareholders’ money in the business).

**Shareholders’ Equity**

Shareholders’ Equity = Total Assets − Total Liabilities

**Formulas**

- ROE (fraction):  
  ROE = Net Income / Shareholders’ Equity

- ROE (percentage):  
  ROE (%) = (Net Income / Shareholders’ Equity) * 100

**Example**

- Net Income           = 10,000,000  
- Shareholders’ Equity = 50,000,000  

ROE = 10,000,000 / 50,000,000 = 0.20 = 20%

**How to Use**

- Investing: Many investors like ROE ≥ 15% and consistently above peers. Be careful if very high ROE is driven by heavy leverage.  
- Trading: Rising ROE trends combined with strong price momentum support medium‑term breakout or trend‑following setups.


### 1.3 Return on Assets (ROA)

**Meaning**  
Return on assets measures how efficiently a company uses its total assets to generate profit.

**Formulas**

- ROA (fraction):  
  ROA = Net Income / Total Assets

- ROA (percentage):  
  ROA (%) = (Net Income / Total Assets) * 100

**Example**

- Net Income   = 10,000,000  
- Total Assets = 100,000,000  

ROA = 10,000,000 / 100,000,000 = 0.10 = 10%

**How to Use**

- Investing: For asset‑heavy firms, prefer ROA above the sector median; even 5–8% can be strong depending on industry.  
- Trading: Deteriorating ROA into earnings or over several quarters can be an early warning that a bullish trend is losing fundamental support.


### 1.4 Gross Margin

**Meaning**  
Gross margin shows how much money is left after direct production costs (cost of goods sold) but before other operating expenses, interest, and tax.

**Formulas**

- Gross Margin (fraction):  
  Gross Margin = (Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) / Revenue

- Gross Margin (percentage):  
  Gross Margin (%) = (Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) / Revenue * 100

**Example**

- Revenue           = 100,000,000  
- Cost of Goods Sold = 60,000,000  

Gross Margin = (100,000,000 − 60,000,000) / 100,000,000  
             = 40,000,000 / 100,000,000  
             = 0.40 = 40%

**How to Use**

- Investing: Look for high and stable or improving gross margins vs peers. Persistent margin compression can signal competitive or cost issues.  
- Trading: Large positive/negative gross‑margin surprises are common triggers for sharp earnings‑day moves.


## 2. Valuation Metrics

### 2.1 Earnings per Share (EPS)

**Meaning**  
Earnings per share represents the profit attributable to each share of common stock (simplified).

**Formula**

EPS = Net Income / Number of Shares Outstanding

**How to Use**

- Investing: Focus on companies with growing EPS over time; check that growth is supported by revenue, not only buybacks.  
- Trading: Big EPS beats or misses vs expectations often drive gaps and trend days.


### 2.2 Price‑to‑Earnings Ratio (P/E)

**Meaning**  
P/E ratio tells you how much the market is willing to pay for one unit of earnings per share.

**Formula**

P/E Ratio = Share Price / Earnings per Share (EPS)

**Example**

- Share Price = 100  
- EPS        = 10  

P/E = 100 / 10 = 10

**How to Use**

- Investing: Compare P/E to (1) industry average and (2) company’s own history. Many value investors prefer P/E < 15–20, but context matters. Very low P/E can signal either undervaluation or fundamental problems.  
- Trading: High P/E with strong growth and momentum can still work short‑term; high P/E plus slowing growth is fragile and prone to sharp corrections.


### 2.3 Price‑to‑Book Ratio (P/B)

**Meaning**  
P/B ratio compares the share price to the book value per share (the accounting value of equity per share).

**Book Value of Equity**

Book Value of Equity = Total Assets − Total Liabilities

**Book Value per Share**

Book Value per Share = (Total Assets − Total Liabilities) / Shares Outstanding

**Formula**

P/B Ratio = Share Price / Book Value per Share

**Example**

- Share Price         = 50  
- Book Value per Share = 25  

P/B = 50 / 25 = 2

**How to Use**

- Investing: For asset‑heavy sectors (banks, insurers, industrials), P/B < 1.0 can point to potential deep value; for high‑intangible tech, P/B is less informative. Always compare with sector norms.  
- Trading: P/B is more of a backdrop; combine low P/B with improving price trend for value‑plus‑momentum ideas.


### 2.4 Price‑to‑Sales Ratio (P/S)

**Meaning**  
P/S ratio compares share price to revenue per share; it is especially useful when earnings are small or volatile.

**Revenue per Share**

Revenue per Share = Total Revenue / Shares Outstanding

**Formula**

P/S Ratio = Share Price / Revenue per Share

**Example**

- Share Price       = 30  
- Revenue per Share = 10  

P/S = 30 / 10 = 3

**How to Use**

- Investing: For growth stocks, investors often like P/S in a “reasonable” range (e.g., below 2–4), but acceptable levels depend heavily on growth and margins.  
- Trading: Very high P/S with hot sentiment can fuel strong momentum but is vulnerable to sharp de‑rates on bad news.


### 2.5 Dividend Yield

**Meaning**  
Dividend yield measures the annual dividend income you receive from a stock relative to the current share price.

**Formulas**

- Dividend Yield (fraction):  
  Dividend Yield = Annual Dividend per Share / Share Price

- Dividend Yield (percentage):  
  Dividend Yield (%) = (Annual Dividend per Share / Share Price) * 100

**Example**

- Annual Dividend per Share = 2  
- Share Price               = 50  

Dividend Yield = 2 / 50 = 0.04 = 4%

**How to Use**

- Investing: Look for yields that are attractive but sustainable. For many sectors, 2–5% with a moderate payout ratio can be healthy. Extremely high yields can indicate distress or an impending cut.  
- Trading: Yield is secondary; more relevant for longer‑term income plays than short‑term trades.


## 3. Growth Metrics

### 3.1 One‑Year EPS Growth

**Meaning**  
EPS growth shows how quickly earnings per share have grown or declined between two periods.

Let:  
EPS0 = EPS in the base year  
EPS1 = EPS in the current year  

**Formula**

EPS Growth (%) = (EPS1 − EPS0) / EPS0 * 100

**How to Use**

- Investing: Favor businesses with consistent positive EPS growth, especially double‑digit growth supported by revenue and healthy balance sheets.  
- Trading: Positive EPS growth and beats versus expectations can fuel breakouts; negative EPS growth makes rallies more fragile.


### 3.2 One‑Year Revenue Growth

**Meaning**  
Revenue growth shows how quickly sales (revenue) have grown between two periods.

Let:  
Revenue0 = Revenue in the base year  
Revenue1 = Revenue in the current year  

**Formula**

Revenue Growth (%) = (Revenue1 − Revenue0) / Revenue0 * 100

**How to Use**

- Investing: For growth names, double‑digit revenue growth is attractive if margins and leverage are reasonable. Zero or negative growth is a warning unless it’s a deep‑value turnaround thesis.  
- Trading: Accelerating revenue growth is a positive tailwind for momentum strategies.


### 3.3 Free Cash Flow (FCF) Growth (One‑Year)

**Meaning**  
FCF growth measures how fast free cash flow is increasing or decreasing across periods.

Let:  
FCF0 = FCF in the base period  
FCF1 = FCF in the current period  

**Formula**

FCF Growth (%) = (FCF1 − FCF0) / FCF0 * 100

**How to Use**

- Investing: Prefer businesses with positive and growing FCF; persistent negative or highly volatile FCF is a concern, especially in mature industries.  
- Trading: Strong FCF growth makes dip‑buying more defensible on quality names; negative FCF increases downside risk.


### 3.4 Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)

**Meaning**  
CAGR is the average annual growth rate of a value over multiple years, assuming the growth is compounded.

Let:  
V0 = Beginning Value  
Vn = Ending Value  
n  = Number of years  

**Formulas**

- CAGR (fraction):  
  CAGR = (Vn / V0)^(1 / n) − 1

- CAGR (percentage):  
  CAGR (%) = ((Vn / V0)^(1 / n) − 1) * 100

**How to Use**

- Investing: Use multi‑year CAGR for revenue, EPS, and FCF to judge whether growth is durable. Higher long‑term CAGR with strong profitability supports long‑term compounding.  
- Trading: Less important for short horizons, but strong historical CAGR increases confidence in buying pullbacks in leaders.


## 4. Cash Flow Statement Structure

### 4.1 Operating Cash Flow

Cash generated or used by regular business activities, such as cash received from customers and cash paid to suppliers, employees, etc.

### 4.2 Investing Cash Flow

Cash generated or used by investing activities, like buying or selling property, equipment, or financial investments.

### 4.3 Financing Cash Flow

Cash generated or used via debt and equity, such as:

- Borrowing money  
- Repaying loans  
- Issuing shares  
- Paying dividends  
- Buying back shares  

### 4.4 Net Cash Flow

Net Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow + Investing Cash Flow + Financing Cash Flow

### 4.5 Ending Cash Balance

Ending Cash = Beginning Cash + Net Cash Flow for the Period


## 5. Free Cash Flow (FCF)

### 5.1 Simple Free Cash Flow from Cash Flow Statement

**Meaning**  
Free cash flow is the cash a company generates from its core operations after spending on capital expenditures (CapEx). It is the cash available to capital providers or, more simply, to equity holders.

**Definitions**

- Operating Cash Flow = Net cash from operating activities  
- Capital Expenditures (CapEx) = Cash spent on property, plant, equipment, etc.

**Formula**

Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditures

**Example**

- Operating Cash Flow = 700,000  
- Capital Expenditures = 300,000  

Free Cash Flow = 700,000 − 300,000 = 400,000

**How to Use**

- Investing: Prefer companies with positive FCF that comfortably covers dividends and buybacks. FCF yield (FCF / Market Cap) of roughly 5–8%+ can be attractive, depending on risk profile.  
- Trading: Positive and stable FCF makes a momentum stock more resilient; negative FCF makes it more vulnerable to macro shocks and liquidity squeezes.


### 5.2 Free Cash Flow (Income‑Statement Approximation)

Sometimes used when only the income statement and balance sheet are available.

**Formula (approximate)**

Free Cash Flow ≈ Net Income  
+ Depreciation and Amortization  
− Changes in Non‑Cash Working Capital  
− Capital Expenditures

Where Change in Non‑Cash Working Capital is typically:  
(Change in Current Assets excluding cash) − (Change in Current Liabilities excluding debt).


## 6. Debt and Capital Structure Ratios

### 6.1 Debt‑to‑Equity Ratio (D/E)

**Meaning**  
Measures how much debt a company uses relative to equity. Higher values mean more leverage and financial risk.

**Formula**

Debt‑to‑Equity Ratio = Total Debt / Total Shareholders’ Equity

(Some analysts use interest‑bearing debt only; others use total liabilities.)

**How to Use**

- Investing: Many prefer D/E < 1 as conservative; 1–2 can be acceptable, while much above 2 becomes risky and very industry‑specific. Avoid businesses with very high and rising D/E plus weak earnings.  
- Trading: Higher D/E increases downside risk during drawdowns or rate shocks; adjust position size accordingly.


### 6.2 Interest Coverage Ratio

**Meaning**  
Shows how many times a company can cover its interest expense with operating profit (EBIT).

**Formula**

Interest Coverage = EBIT / Interest Expense

Where:  
EBIT = Earnings Before Interest and Taxes

**How to Use**

- Investing: Interest coverage above about 3x is often considered comfortable; low or falling coverage is a red flag, especially if interest rates are rising.  
- Trading: Poor coverage makes a stock more vulnerable to bad macro news or credit events.


## 7. Liquidity Ratios

### 7.1 Current Ratio

**Meaning**  
Measures the ability to pay short‑term obligations with short‑term assets.

**Formula**

Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities

**How to Use**

- Investing: Many view 1.2–2.0 as a “healthy” range. Below 1 can indicate potential liquidity stress; far above 2 might mean inefficient use of assets. Always compare with sector norms.  
- Trading: Low current ratio plus negative news or earnings can produce aggressive selloffs.


### 7.2 Quick Ratio (Acid‑Test)

**Meaning**  
Stricter than the current ratio; excludes inventory from assets.

**Formula**

Quick Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory) / Current Liabilities

**How to Use**

- Investing: Quick ratio ≥ 1 is often considered comfortable; substantially below 1 can be a warning for cash‑tight businesses.  
- Trading: Very low quick ratio increases the risk that bad news triggers sharp moves, especially in small caps.


## 8. Efficiency Ratios (Turnover)

### 8.1 Inventory Turnover

**Meaning**  
Shows how many times inventory is sold and replaced over a period. Higher can reflect efficient inventory management.

**Formula**

Inventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold / Average Inventory

Average Inventory = (Beginning Inventory + Ending Inventory) / 2

**How to Use**

- Investing: Compare with peers. Too low may indicate overstocking/obsolescence; too high may suggest under‑stocking or supply issues.  
- Trading: Sharp deterioration in inventory turnover can foreshadow weaker demand and unreliable rallies.


### 8.2 Receivables Turnover

**Meaning**  
Shows how efficiently the company collects cash from customers.

**Formula**

Receivables Turnover = Net Credit Sales / Average Accounts Receivable

Average Accounts Receivable = (Beginning A/R + Ending A/R) / 2

**How to Use**

- Investing: Higher turnover than peers usually indicates efficient collections; a steep decline can signal deteriorating customer quality or credit risk.  
- Trading: Weak receivables metrics plus cautious management commentary can accelerate downtrends.


## 9. Enterprise‑Value‑Based Metrics

### 9.1 Enterprise Value (EV)

**Meaning**  
Enterprise value represents the total value of the business to all capital providers (debt + equity − cash).

**Formulas**

Market Capitalization = Share Price * Shares Outstanding

Enterprise Value = Market Capitalization  
                   + Total Debt  
                   − Cash and Cash Equivalents

**How to Use**

- Investing: EV is the base for EV/EBITDA, EV/FCF and other “whole business” valuation metrics, which can be more comparable across different capital structures.  
- Trading: High EV versus fundamentals can signal vulnerability if growth disappoints.


### 9.2 EV / EBITDA

**Meaning**  
Compares enterprise value to EBITDA; often used as a valuation multiple across companies.

**Formula**

EV / EBITDA = Enterprise Value / EBITDA

Where:  
EBITDA = Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization

**How to Use**

- Investing: Lower EV/EBITDA than sector peers often indicates a cheaper stock, all else equal. Many mature businesses cluster somewhere around 8–12x, but this varies by industry.  
- Trading: Very rich EV/EBITDA with slowing growth can lead to sharp multiple compression on negative catalysts.


## 10. Per‑Share and Payout Metrics

### 10.1 Cash Flow per Share

**Meaning**  
Cash generated from operations attributable to each share (simplified).

**Formula**

Cash Flow per Share = Operating Cash Flow / Shares Outstanding

**How to Use**

- Investing: Prefer companies with a clear uptrend in cash flow per share; it supports earnings quality, dividends, and buybacks.  
- Trading: Strong and rising cash flow per share makes a name more resilient to volatility.


### 10.2 Dividend Payout Ratio

**Meaning**  
Shows what portion of earnings is paid out as dividends.

**Formulas**

- Using per‑share data:  
  Dividend Payout Ratio = Dividends per Share / Earnings per Share

- Using totals:  
  Dividend Payout Ratio = Total Dividends / Net Income

**How to Use**

- Investing: Many income investors like payout ratios in the ~30–60% range. Very high payout (80–90%+) can be risky unless the business is extremely stable (e.g., certain utilities/REITs).  
- Trading: Unsustainably high payout plus weak earnings increases the probability of a dividend cut and sharp selling.

---

## 11. Practical Overview

- For **long‑term investing**, you typically want:  
  - Strong profitability (ROE, margins)  
  - Positive and growing FCF  
  - Reasonable valuations vs sector (P/E, EV/EBITDA, etc.)  
  - Moderate leverage and solid liquidity  
- For **short‑term trading**, fundamentals help you:  
  - Filter for higher‑quality names to trade  
  - Avoid landmines: very high debt, weak liquidity, negative FCF, collapsing margins  
  - Understand which earnings reports are more likely to move price strongly.
